When Grammar Learns to Respect Women

How patriarchy hides inside our sentences — and how Malayalam quietly refuses it

Aadil Samjeed Jan 11, 2026 · 5 min read

Language is not just how we speak. It is how power trains us to think. Every sentence we form carries invisible assumptions about who matters, who leads, and who is allowed to simply exist without explanation. We often look for sexism in laws, customs, and institutions, but some of the most stubborn forms of inequality live quietly inside grammar itself.

Every time we say “he,” “she,” “went,” or “did,” we are rehearsing an old worldview.

Most of the world’s major languages were shaped in deeply patriarchal societies, and they still wear that history. In English, a man is simply Mr., unchanged by marriage or status. A woman must be Miss, Mrs., or Ms., her identity filtered through her relationship to a man. In many languages, professions are split into male and female forms, as if doing the same job somehow becomes different when a woman does it.

But the deepest bias hides in verbs.

1. When Actions Are Forced to Have a Gender

In Hindi, the pronoun “वह” (vah) is used for both “he” and “she.” On the surface, that looks neutral. But the verb tells the real story:

वह गया — he went
वह गई — she went

The action itself changes based on gender. A man does not simply go; he goes in a masculine way. A woman does not simply go; she goes in a feminine way. Walking, leaving, moving — all of it is forced to wear gender.

The language hides gender in the pronoun and then stamps it onto the action. It quietly teaches the mind that masculinity and femininity are not just identities — they are properties of motion itself.

This is not neutrality. It is patriarchy in grammatical form.

• • •

2. Malayalam’s Quiet Rebellion

Now look at Malayalam.

അവൻ പോയി — he went
അവൾ പോയി — she went

Here, gender appears in the pronoun, where it belongs — identity is personal, chosen, intimate. But the verb?

പോയി (poyi) never changes.

The action is free from gender.
A man goes.
A woman goes.
A non-binary person goes.

Going is just going.

Malayalam makes a subtle but powerful philosophical choice: identity belongs to the person, but action belongs to humanity.

That single grammatical decision separates Malayalam from most patriarchal languages on earth.

• • •

3. Jobs Without Gender Cages

The same logic shows up in professions. English fractures roles into male and female — actor and actress, waiter and waitress. Many Indian languages do the same. The masculine form becomes the default; the feminine becomes a variation.

Malayalam often resists this. ഡോക്ടർ, അധ്യാപകർ, എഴുത്തുകാരൻ — the role comes first. Gender can be added, but it is not required. The language allows a woman to be a doctor without turning her into a special grammatical category.

It sounds small. It is not.
It means what you do is more important than what you are.

4. Why Malayalam Ended Up Different

Kerala was never a feminist paradise. Patriarchy has always existed here — in Malabar, in Travancore, everywhere. Women were expected to manage the home, raise children, stay modest, and depend on male income. Men were cast as providers. Women were cast as dependents. Control over women’s mobility, sexuality, and choices was real and often brutal.

That lived memory, passed down through mothers and grandmothers, is true.

But Kerala also carried something rare in South Asia: pockets of matrilineal tradition. Among Nairs, Ezhavas, and some Muslim and tribal communities, property and lineage passed through women. A husband came to a wife’s home. Children belonged to the mother’s family line.

This did not remove male power — men still dominated politics, religion, and public authority — but it disrupted one crucial assumption: that women were economically and genealogically disposable.

So Kerala lived inside a contradiction.

Socially patriarchal.
Structurally not entirely.

Women could be controlled — but not erased. They could be subordinated — but not fully uprooted.

Languages grow from such tensions. Malayalam absorbed this mixed reality. It did not become feminist, but it did not collapse into pure male-default grammar either. It left a small grammatical space where women could remain subjects, not just extensions. And that space became everything.

5. Why This Matters Beyond Grammar

Language decides what feels natural. If every sentence reminds you that women are different, secondary, marked, society follows. If a language lets women walk, work, speak, and leave without turning every action into a gendered performance, it creates friction against patriarchy.

That friction is where dignity grows.

Malayalam does not shout equality. It simply refuses to make masculinity the default state of being.

Most languages turn men into humans and women into footnotes.

Malayalam, shaped by a complicated and contradictory history, quietly does something rarer.

It lets people act — before it lets them be judged by gender.

It is grammar learning how to respect women.
