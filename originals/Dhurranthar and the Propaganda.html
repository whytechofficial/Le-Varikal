Dhurandhar and the New Architecture of Cinematic Propaganda

Voice. Vision. Verse.

Cinema rarely declares its politics openly. It smuggles them in through emotion.

A speech can be debated. A manifesto can be challenged. But a powerful story—wrapped in spectacle, music and heroism—slips quietly past the mind’s defenses. By the time the audience realizes what it has absorbed, the message is already sitting comfortably inside memory.

This is where propaganda becomes most effective.

Modern political cinema rarely functions through a single technique. Instead, it relies on a set of narrative patterns—fictionalized crises, simplified enemies, selective history and emotional nationalism. Each pattern alone may appear harmless. But together they create stories that feel less like entertainment and more like emotional lessons about the nation.

Dhurandhar appears to operate within precisely this territory. Its premise relies on a simple psychological loophole: fiction grants permission. When a film declares itself fictional, viewers instinctively lower their guard. Ideology can then move through the narrative almost unnoticed.

Aditya Dhar seems to understand this mechanism well.

Dhurandhar does not claim to depict real events. Instead, it constructs a fictional national crisis that feels eerily familiar. The antagonists resemble recognizable geopolitical enemies. The rhetoric echoes modern nationalist discourse. The stakes mirror the anxieties that dominate television debates and social media timelines.

Nothing is explicitly real. Yet everything feels real enough to be believed. And that feeling is where the film’s power lies.

From Historical Claim to Fictional Camouflage

Dhar’s earlier film Uri: The Surgical Strike relied heavily on documented history. The surgical strikes were real military operations, and the film dramatized them as a triumph of national courage.

Reality gave the story credibility.

Once audiences accepted that credibility, the ideological messaging travelled easily. The famous line “ghar mein ghus kar maarenge” became more than cinematic dialogue—it was celebrated as a declaration of patriotic resolve.

The emotional logic was simple: aggression equals strength, and strength equals patriotism.

But Dhurandhar signals a strategic shift.

Dhar appears to have realized that fiction can be a stronger shield than historical claim. When a film claims to portray real events, critics can challenge its accuracy. When a film declares itself fictional, criticism becomes easier to dismiss.

A disclaimer becomes armour.

By stating that Dhurandhar is fictional, Dhar gains narrative freedom. The film can echo real conflicts and reproduce familiar political narratives while remaining immune to factual scrutiny.

The Padmaavat Template

A similar phenomenon occurred with Padmaavat.

The film was based on a fictional poem written by Malik Muhammad Jayasi, yet its grand visual style convinced many viewers that they were witnessing genuine medieval history. The cinematic depiction of Alauddin Khalji became embedded in popular imagination despite lacking strong historical grounding.

The power of the film did not lie in historical accuracy.

It lay in how convincingly the story resembled history.

Cinema has the ability to overwrite public memory.

Once a story settles into the imagination, correcting it becomes extremely difficult.

The Emotional Engineering of Propaganda

Political propaganda in cinema rarely appears as argument. It appears as emotion.

Films like Dhurandhar construct simplified moral worlds where the audience instinctively knows who the heroes are and who the villains must be.

Several techniques help create this emotional architecture:

Heroic Militarisation

The soldier becomes the moral centre of the story. Violence is framed as sacrifice and protection rather than aggression.

Simplified Enemies

Complex geopolitical realities are reduced to easily recognizable villains.

Permanent National Crisis

The nation is portrayed as constantly under threat, creating urgency and discouraging skepticism.

These techniques encourage viewers to experience politics as instinct rather than analysis.

Additional Propaganda Pattern 1: Selective History

Another recurring pattern in modern Bollywood is selective historical storytelling.

Films highlight moments that reinforce national pride while quietly excluding historical complexity. This produces narratives in which the nation appears morally pure and eternally heroic.

A notable example is The Kashmir Files. The film focuses on the tragic displacement of Kashmiri Pandits—an undeniably real trauma. However, critics argue that its narrative simplifies a deeply complex conflict into a single emotional perspective.

Similarly, Samrat Prithviraj portrays medieval history as a clear civilizational struggle between heroic Hindu rulers and invading Muslim forces, leaving little room for historical nuance.

Selective storytelling does not necessarily fabricate history. It curates it.

Additional Propaganda Pattern 2: The Manufactured Enemy

Modern political cinema also frequently constructs a clear external enemy. This narrative device simplifies complex geopolitical tensions by turning them into recognizable villains.

In many Indian action and spy films, Pakistan often occupies this role. Movies such as Pathaan and similar thrillers frame the conflict through a familiar structure: the Indian hero defending the nation against a ruthless Pakistani adversary.

India and Pakistan do share a long and real geopolitical rivalry. But repeated cinematic portrayals gradually turn that rivalry into a simplified moral narrative.

The enemy becomes less a political actor and more a symbolic villain.

Over time, repetition can produce a subtle psychological effect. When audiences repeatedly encounter a particular national identity associated with threat or violence, the association begins to feel natural. The line between political conflict and cultural suspicion can blur.

In some narratives, portrayals of hostile external forces occasionally overlap with coded suspicion toward Indian Muslims—through accents, imagery or character design that links domestic identity with foreign antagonists.

Most viewers may not consciously notice this connection. Yet repeated storytelling patterns can slowly reinforce emotional associations.

Cinema rarely creates prejudice on its own. But it can amplify and normalize existing suspicions by presenting them in dramatic and emotionally satisfying forms.

Identity and Narrative Authority

Part of Dhar’s credibility in the public imagination also comes from his identity as a Kashmiri Pandit. In public discussions and interviews, this identity often functions as a marker of authenticity when speaking about Kashmir.

Personal experience can offer powerful insight. Yet when cinema elevates one experience into a dominant national narrative while excluding others, storytelling begins to resemble political messaging.

Kashmir’s history contains multiple traumas and perspectives.

Reducing it to a single voice risks turning history into ideology.

The Psychology of Narrative Persuasion

The effectiveness of propaganda in storytelling is explained by Narrative Transportation Theory, developed by media psychologists such as Melanie C. Green and Timothy C. Brock.

The theory suggests that when audiences become deeply immersed in a story, they temporarily suspend disbelief and critical thinking. Emotional engagement replaces analytical judgment.

In that state, viewers are far more likely to accept the assumptions embedded in the narrative.

The audience does not feel persuaded. They simply feel entertained.

Cinema in a Polarised Climate

The political impact of films like Dhurandhar cannot be separated from the environment in which they appear.

India’s contemporary media landscape is intensely polarized. Television debates, social media discourse and electoral rhetoric constantly circulate emotionally charged narratives about national identity and security.

In such a climate, cinematic stories easily merge with public perception of reality.

Films begin to shape common sense—the intuitive understanding people carry about the nation and its enemies.

Fiction as the Perfect Disguise

This is why Dhurandhar matters.

The film may insist it is fictional. Yet its emotional narrative mirrors real political anxieties closely enough that audiences interpret it as commentary on contemporary conflicts.

The most effective propaganda rarely announces itself.

It hides inside thrilling stories, heroic characters and satisfying emotional conclusions. Entertainment becomes camouflage.

The Evolution of Aditya Dhar’s Method

In Uri, ideology travelled through history.

In Dhurandhar, ideology travels through fiction.

This shift represents a more sophisticated stage of political filmmaking. Fiction provides plausible deniability while still shaping emotional perception.

The audience leaves the theatre believing it has simply watched a gripping story.

But the story quietly leaves behind a framework for interpreting reality.

The Final Problem

Cinema in a charged political atmosphere is never neutral. It participates in shaping how a nation imagines itself—who belongs, who threatens, and who deserves suspicion.

Dhurandhar may insist that it is only fiction. But fiction does not stay inside the theatre.

It travels into conversations, into memory, into the quiet assumptions people carry about their neighbours and their country.

The danger is not that cinema tells lies. The danger is that it tells them beautifully enough that they begin to feel like truth.
