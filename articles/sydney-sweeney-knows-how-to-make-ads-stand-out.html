Sydney Sweeney Knows How to Make Ads Stand Out

Gender & Society·English·Le Varikal

Some celebrities appear in advertisements.

Sydney Sweeney appears in conversations.

In September 2026, the sports prediction platform Novig launched its “Just Sports” campaign with Sweeney as its face — and, notably, as an equity holder who helped shape the campaign herself. The visuals were impossible to scroll past: Sweeney nearly nude, her modesty preserved only by strategically placed footballs, tennis rackets, hockey pads and a catcher’s helmet, beamed across Times Square billboards. Her line in the commercial: “Novig is just sports. So they wanted to show just sports.”

It was bold. It was also something else.

But honestly, the way the campaign chose to portray women in sport is something I don’t agree with. There is a difference between making an advertisement bold and reducing the image of women in sport to something sexualised — and “Just Sports” walked straight past that line. The concept wasn’t incidental styling; it was the entire idea. Take the iconography of sport, remove the athletes, and drape a nude celebrity in the gear.

The reaction from female athletes made that difference especially clear.

Four-time Olympic swimming champion Ariarne Titmus called it “a kick in the face” to women who dedicate themselves to sport. Former UCLA gymnast Gracie Kramer posted footage of herself competing with a simple caption: this is what a woman in sport looks like. Australian Olympian Tilly Kearns called it “a step backwards.” British sprinter Amy Hunt, boxer Skye Nicolson and others joined in — athletes across countries and codes, all making the same point: the ad asks viewers to appraise desirability, not competence.

Even Sharon Stone weighed in, saying Sweeney should take responsibility for her actions: “We can’t denigrate women’s sports to get men’s attention so they can gamble.” In Kolkata, East Bengal FC answered with images of its own women footballers in action — no glamour, just the game.

Novig’s CEO insisted the company is “hugely supportive of women’s sports” and never intended Sweeney to represent female athletes. Sweeney herself pointed to old provocative shoots of Serena Williams and Ronda Rousey, as if precedent settles the argument. It doesn’t. There is a difference between an athlete choosing how to present her own body and a betting brand borrowing a celebrity’s to sell prediction markets.

And yet, there is another part of this story that is difficult to ignore.

The advertisement worked.

Not necessarily because everyone liked it. Quite the opposite.

Within five days it had nearly 39 million views. Brand mentions multiplied ninefold. The BBC and The New York Times covered a betting app’s commercial. Female athletes responded. Social media debated it. News outlets covered it. The advertisement escaped the boundaries of the brand that created it and became part of a much larger cultural conversation.

But here is the detail that complicates the victory lap: only about 4% of those social posts mentioned the Novig app itself. Billions of impressions, and almost nobody was talking about the product. Reach was achieved. Whether it becomes revenue is a question the numbers don’t answer yet.

And this isn’t the first time Sweeney has done that.

American Eagle’s “Sydney Sweeney Has Great Jeans” campaign in July 2025 created its own controversy, particularly because of its deliberate jeans/genes wordplay and the questions it raised about beauty, heredity and representation. Critics heard an uncomfortable echo in it — the old idea that good genes are something you inherit, and that some people inherit better ones. The brand got its conversation. What it didn’t clearly get was a commercial payoff to match the noise.

The two campaigns are completely different. But there is one striking similarity: Sweeney’s presence turns the advertisement into something people feel compelled to discuss.

That doesn’t mean every campaign deserves praise. It doesn’t mean controversy automatically makes an advertisement good. And in the case of the Novig campaign, I think the criticism from women in sport deserves to be taken seriously — because it comes from the people who actually live inside the world the ad was borrowing.

But from an advertising perspective, there is something fascinating happening here.

Sweeney doesn’t simply appear in a campaign and disappear when the commercial ends. She isn’t a passive face hired to smile beside a product — in Novig’s case she was literally a part-owner of the controversy, shaping the visuals herself. Her image becomes the centre of the discussion. The product attaches itself to the conversation. And the conversation keeps the advertisement alive long after the original campaign has been released.

From American Eagle to Novig, the pattern keeps appearing:

Attention. Reaction. Debate. Conversation.

Whether the reaction is admiration, criticism or outrage, the brand has achieved something increasingly difficult in modern advertising — getting people to stop scrolling.

The open question is what the stopping is worth. Attention is the cheapest currency on the internet and the hardest to convert into anything real. Outrage is a sugar high: intense, fast, and gone by morning. The brands that win the conversation don’t always win the customer.

Sydney Sweeney doesn’t just enter campaigns.

She enters the conversation.
